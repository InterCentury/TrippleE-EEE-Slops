# 10.02 - Motor Drivers: Silicon Switching, H-Bridge Topologies, and Current Regulation

## 1. The Necessity of Power Isolation

Microcontrollers are logic devices, not power delivery mechanisms. A typical modern microcontroller General-Purpose Input/Output (GPIO) pin can source or sink an absolute maximum of **20 mA to 40 mA** at 3.3V or 5.0V.

Conversely, a standard small DC motor might require **500 mA to 2000 mA (2A)** under load, operating at voltages between 6V and 24V. Connecting a motor directly to an MCU pin will instantly destroy the internal silicon traces due to severe overcurrent and thermal runaway.

To bridge the logic domain and the power domain, engineers utilize **Motor Drivers**—solid-state amplification bridges that take low-voltage, low-current control signals and use them to gate high-voltage, high-current power rails.

---

## 2. The Complete H-Bridge Architecture

An H-Bridge is the fundamental circuit topology required to drive a brushed DC motor in both forward and reverse directions using a unipolar (single positive) power supply. It utilizes four independent solid-state switches arranged symmetrically.

```text
> COMPLETE H-BRIDGE TOPOLOGY WITH FLYBACK DIODES

      External Motor Supply Rail (V_MOT: e.g., 12V)
        │                                     │
      ┌─┴─┐                                 ┌─┴─┐
      │ Q1│ (High-Side FET)                 │ Q3│ (High-Side FET)
      └─┬─┘                                 └─┬─┘
  D1  ▲ │                                     │ ▲ D3
 (Rev)│ ├───┐                             ┌───┤ │(Rev)
      │ │   │        [ DC Motor ]         │   │ │
      ├─┘   ├────────── ( M ) ────────────┤   └─┤
      │     │  (+)                   (-)  │     │
      │ ├───┘                             └───┤ │
  D2  ▲ │                                     │ ▲ D4
 (Rev)│ │                                     │ │(Rev)
      ┌─┴─┐                                 ┌─┴─┐
      │ Q2│ (Low-Side FET)                  │ Q4│ (Low-Side FET)
      └─┬─┘                                 └─┬─┘
        │                                     │
    Ground / Shunt Resistor Return Path (GND)

```

### 2.1 H-Bridge Commutation Truth Table

The physical state of the motor is dictated entirely by which diagonal pair of switches are driven into conduction (closed).

| State | Q1 (High-L) | Q2 (Low-L) | Q3 (High-R) | Q4 (Low-R) | Motor Behavior |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Forward** | ON | OFF | OFF | ON | Current flows Left-to-Right. Motor spins CW. |
| **Reverse** | OFF | ON | ON | OFF | Current flows Right-to-Left. Motor spins CCW. |
| **Coast** | OFF | OFF | OFF | OFF | Motor disconnected. Shaft free-spins to a stop. |
| **Brake** | OFF | ON | OFF | ON | Both low-sides ON. Motor shorted, dynamic braking. |
| **Shoot-Through** | ON | ON | *Any* | *Any* | **CATASTROPHIC FAULT.** Direct short from V_MOT to GND. |

### 2.2 Dead-Time Insertion

When transitioning from Forward to Reverse, Q1 must turn off and Q2 must turn on. However, silicon transistors do not turn off instantly; they have microscopic capacitance that takes time to discharge. If Q2 turns on while Q1 is still partially conducting, a momentary short-circuit (**Shoot-Through**) occurs.

To prevent this, advanced motor drivers inject **Dead-Time**—a microscopic delay (typically 100 ns to 500 ns) where *all* switches are forced off before the next pair is turned on.

## 3. Inductive Kickback and Flyback Protection

A motor is not a simple resistor; it is a massive inductor. When current flows through the copper coils of the motor, a strong magnetic field is generated. When the H-Bridge switches suddenly turn OFF (e.g., during PWM cycling), this magnetic field collapses instantly.

### 3.1 The $di/dt$ Catastrophe

According to Faraday's Law of Induction, an inductor opposes sudden changes in current by generating a massive reverse voltage spike:

$$V_L = L \frac{di}{dt}$$

Where:

* $V_L$ = **Induced Voltage Spike**, measured in Volts ($\text{V}$).
* $L$ = **Inductance of the Motor Coils**, measured in Henrys ($\text{H}$).
* $di/dt$ = **Rate of change of current over time**.

If the switches open near-instantly ($dt \approx 0$), the induced voltage ($V_L$) approaches infinity. In reality, it will easily hit hundreds of volts, instantly punching through the thin silicon of the transistors and destroying the driver chip.

### 3.2 Flyback Diodes (Freewheeling Diodes)

To safely dissipate this energy, four **Flyback Diodes** (D1 through D4 in the schematic above) are placed in parallel with the transistors, pointing "backward" (reverse-biased during normal operation).

When the switch opens, the massive negative voltage spike forward-biases the diodes, providing a safe, closed loop for the current to recirculate and dissipate through the motor's own internal coil resistance ($I^2R$ loss) without harming the logic switches.

## 4. Bipolar Junction Transistors (BJT) vs. MOSFETs

Historically, motor drivers (like the ubiquitous **L298N**) were built using Bipolar Junction Transistors (BJTs). Modern drivers (like the **TB6612FNG** or **DRV8833**) are built using Metal-Oxide-Semiconductor Field-Effect Transistors (MOSFETs).

### 4.1 BJT Drivers (e.g., L298N)

BJTs operate via current amplification and suffer from a fixed voltage drop across the transistor junction when fully saturated ($V_{CE(\text{sat})}$), usually around 1.0V to 1.5V per transistor.
Because current must pass through two transistors in an H-bridge (one high-side, one low-side), the total voltage lost to the driver is:

$$V_{\text{drop\_total}} = V_{CE(\text{sat\_high})} + V_{CE(\text{sat\_low})} \approx 2.0\text{V to } 3.0\text{V}$$

This voltage is converted directly into waste heat.

$$P_{\text{loss}} = V_{\text{drop\_total}} \times I_{\text{motor}}$$

*Example:* Driving a 2A motor results in $P = 2.5\text{V} \times 2\text{A} = 5.0\text{ Watts}$ of pure heat. This is why L298N modules require massive aluminum heatsinks and deliver less voltage to the motor than the battery provides.

### 4.2 MOSFET Drivers (e.g., TB6612FNG)

MOSFETs act as voltage-controlled variable resistors. When fully ON, they possess an incredibly low static resistance known as $R_{DS(\text{on})}$ (typically $0.05\text{ }\Omega$ to $0.2\text{ }\Omega$).

The power dissipated is calculated using Joule heating:

$$P_{\text{loss}} = I_{\text{motor}}^2 \times (R_{DS(\text{on\_high})} + R_{DS(\text{on\_low})})$$

*Example:* Driving a 2A motor through MOSFETs with $0.1\text{ }\Omega$ resistance results in $P = (2^2) \times (0.2) = 0.8\text{ Watts}$ of heat. This allows MOSFET drivers to be tiny, highly efficient, and require no heavy heatsinks.

## 5. PWM Switching Dynamics and Audible Noise

When controlling motor speed via Pulse Width Modulation (PWM), the frequency of the PWM signal drastically affects mechanical performance and human comfort.

### 5.1 Acoustic Resonance (The "PWM Whine")

If the MCU's PWM timer is set between **20 Hz and 20,000 Hz** (20 kHz), the physical coils inside the motor will electromagnetically vibrate at that exact frequency. Because this falls within the human hearing spectrum, the motor will emit an audible, high-pitched "whine" or "squeal".

**Solution:** Modern driver firmware configures hardware timers to generate PWM signals **above 20 kHz** (e.g., 25 kHz to 32 kHz). This pushes the electromagnetic resonance into the ultrasonic band, rendering the motor completely silent to human ears while maintaining precise speed control.

### 5.2 Current Ripple Calculus

PWM does not deliver smooth continuous voltage; it delivers violent ON/OFF square pulses. The motor's inductance acts as an electrical flywheel, smoothing these pulses into an average continuous current. However, a small jagged variation remains, known as **Current Ripple ($\Delta I$)**.

$$\Delta I = \frac{(V_{\text{MOT}} - V_{\text{BEMF}}) \cdot D}{L \cdot f_{\text{PWM}}}$$

Where:

* $V_{\text{MOT}}$ = Supply Voltage.
* $V_{\text{BEMF}}$ = Back-Electromotive Force generated by the spinning rotor.
* $D$ = PWM Duty Cycle (fraction from 0 to 1).
* $L$ = Motor coil inductance.
* $f_{\text{PWM}}$ = PWM switching frequency.

**Key Takeaway:** Higher PWM frequencies ($f_{\text{PWM}}$) decrease current ripple, resulting in smoother torque delivery and less internal motor heating.

## 6. Current Sensing and Active Chopping (Constant Current Control)

For high-performance applications (especially Stepper motors, where coil resistance is extremely low), applying full battery voltage will pull massive, destructive currents. Advanced drivers use **Active Current Limiting** (Chopper Drives).

### 6.1 Shunt Resistor Mathematics

A very small, precise resistor (e.g., $R_{\text{shunt}} = 0.1\text{ }\Omega$) is placed between the low-side of the H-bridge and ground. All motor current ($I_{\text{motor}}$) must flow through this shunt.

```text
  [ Low-Side FET ]
         │
         ├─────► V_sense (To Driver Internal Comparator)
         │
   [ R_shunt ] (e.g., 0.1 Ohms)
         │
        GND

```

Using Ohm's Law, the driver chip continuously measures the voltage drop across the shunt:

$$V_{\text{sense}} = I_{\text{motor}} \times R_{\text{shunt}}$$

### 6.2 Chopper Logic Execution

The developer configures a reference voltage ($V_{\text{ref}}$) on the driver board via a tiny tuning potentiometer.

1. The driver turns the H-Bridge ON. Current ramps up rapidly through the motor coil.
2. The internal comparator monitors $V_{\text{sense}}$.
3. The moment $V_{\text{sense}}$ exceeds $V_{\text{ref}}$, the driver instantly turns the H-Bridge OFF (chopping the current).
4. The current recirculates and decays. After a fixed time, the driver turns the bridge back ON.

This allows a 24V supply to safely drive a motor rated for 3V, guaranteeing maximum acceleration (due to the high voltage overcoming inductance) while strictly capping the peak current to prevent thermal destruction.

## 7. Summary Summary Checklist

1. **Power Isolation Imperative:** MCUs handle milliwatts of logic data; Motor Drivers handle Watts/Kilowatts of kinetic power. They must be physically isolated.
2. **H-Bridge Architecture:** Four independent switches allow bidirectional current flow across a motor from a single unipolar battery source.
3. **Shoot-Through Prevention:** Dead-time must be explicitly engineered into switching state transitions to prevent catastrophic $V_{\text{CC}}$-to-GND short circuits.
4. **Inductive Kickback Dangers:** Collapsing magnetic fields induce massive $V = L(di/dt)$ voltage spikes that will destroy silicon gates.
5. **Flyback Recirculation:** Reverse-biased flyback diodes safely clamp inductive spikes, allowing current to safely loop and decay through the motor winding.
6. **BJT vs. MOSFET Efficiency:** Legacy BJT drivers (L298N) drop up to 3V as waste heat. Modern MOSFET drivers use extremely low $R_{DS(\text{on})}$ channels to achieve high efficiency with zero heatsinking.
7. **Ultrasonic PWM Frequencies:** Pushing PWM timer frequencies above $20\text{ kHz}$ eliminates audible coil whine and reduces destructive current ripple.
8. **Shunt Resistor Acquisition:** Low-ohm shunt resistors placed at the ground return path translate high motor currents into safe, measurable analog voltages.
9. **Active Chopping Limits:** Comparator circuits use shunt feedback to instantly sever H-bridge power when current exceeds pre-set safety thresholds, allowing high-voltage supplies on low-voltage motors.
10. **Dynamic Braking Tactics:** Shorting both low-side switches of an H-bridge turns the kinetic energy of the spinning rotor into a heavy electrical load, halting the motor far faster than passive coasting.
