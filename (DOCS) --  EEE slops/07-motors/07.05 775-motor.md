## 1. Introduction and Structural Topology

The **775 motor** is an industry-standard, high-performance brushed DC motor size class. It is widely used in power tools (such as cordless drills), linear actuators, robotics, and small industrial machinery due to its high power-to-size ratio and high torque output.

The designation "775" refers to its physical form factor dimensions standardized by manufacturers. It features a heavy-duty steel shell housing an internal cooling fan, dual ball bearings, and a high-density wire-wound armature.

## 2. Electromechanical Principles of Operation

The 775 motor operates as a permanent-magnet brushed DC motor (PMDC). Its execution follows **Lorentz Force Mechanics** and mechanical brush commutation:

$$F = I \cdot (L \times B)$$

* $F$ = **Lorentz force** exerted on the rotor copper windings, measured in Newtons ($\text{N}$).
* $I$ = **Armature current** flowing through the wire turns, measured in Amperes ($\text{A}$).
* $L$ = **Active length vector** of the winding wire within the magnetic field, measured in meters ($\text{m}$).
* $B$ = **Magnetic flux density vector** generated by the high-grade internal stator permanent magnets, measured in Tesla ($\text{T}$).
* $\times$ = **Vector cross product operator**, showing that the resulting force is perpendicular to both the wire direction and the magnetic flux lines.

When direct current is applied to the rear terminals, it passes through spring-loaded carbon brushes into the copper split-ring commutator. The commutator switches the current direction inside the spinning armature loops every $180^\circ$ of rotation. This ensures that the generated mechanical force always acts in a single rotational direction, creating continuous shaft torque.

## 3. Engineering Performance Profiles: Pros and Cons

### 3.1 Advantages

* **High Power Density:** Capable of delivering anywhere from $50\text{W}$ to over $350\text{W}$ of mechanical power from a compact, handheld frame size.
* **Dual Ball Bearings:** Premium 775 variants include front and rear ball bearings to handle high radial loads and reduce mechanical friction at high RPMs.
* **Integrated Cooling Fan:** Features an internal centrifugal fan blade that draws air through housing vents, allowing the motor to run at higher current limits without immediate thermal failure.
* **Massive Low-End Torque:** Delivers exceptional stall torque, making it highly effective for applications requiring sudden, high-force starting loads.
* **Low Acquisition Cost:** Simple mechanical design makes it highly economical compared to brushless alternatives of equivalent power outputs.

### 3.2 Disadvantages

* **Physical Component Wear:** The mechanical carbon brushes gradually wear down over time due to friction, limiting total operating lifespan.
* **Electromagnetic Interference (EMI):** Continuous switching across the copper segments generates electrical arcing, which broadcasts radio-frequency noise and requires decoupling capacitors to suppress.
* **Thermal Dissipation Limitations:** Because the high-current coils are located on the internal spinning rotor rather than the outside frame, heat can build up quickly under sustained heavy loads if airflow drops.
* **Acoustic Noise:** The combined friction of the carbon brushes and the rushing sound of the high-speed internal cooling fan makes it significantly louder than brushless designs.

## 4. Electrical and Mechanical Wattage Calculations

To analyze a 775 motor's performance or size an appropriate power supply, engineers track both its electrical input power and mechanical output power.

### 4.1 Electrical Input Power ($P_{\text{in}}$)

The total electrical wattage consumed by the motor from its DC source is calculated using the input voltage and current draw:

$$P_{\text{in}} = V_s \cdot I_{\text{line}}$$

* $P_{\text{in}}$ = **Electrical input power**, measured in Watts ($\text{W}$).
* $V_s$ = **Supply voltage** applied across the motor terminals, measured in Volts ($\text{V}$).
* $I_{\text{line}}$ = **Line current** drawn from the power source, measured in Amperes ($\text{A}$).

### 4.2 Mechanical Output Power ($P_{\text{out}}$)

The actual rotational power delivered through the output shaft is a function of its mechanical torque and rotational velocity:

$$P_{\text{out}} = \tau \cdot \omega$$

* $P_{\text{out}}$ = **Mechanical output power**, measured in Watts ($\text{W}$).
* $\tau$ = **Mechanical torque** produced at the shaft, measured in Newton-meters ($\text{N}\cdot\text{m}$).
* $\omega$ = **Angular velocity** of the shaft, measured in radians per second ($\text{rad/s}$).

To convert standard nameplate speeds from Revolutions Per Minute ($\text{RPM}$) to radians per second, use the following conversion formula:

$$\omega = \frac{\text{RPM} \cdot 2\pi}{60}$$

* $\text{RPM}$ = **Rotational speed** of the shaft, measured in revolutions per minute.
* $\pi$ = **Pi**, the mathematical constant (approximately $3.14159$).
* $60$ = **Time conversion factor**, translating minutes to seconds.

### 4.3 Total Power Efficiency ($\eta$)

Because energy is lost as heat due to internal winding resistance ($I^2R$ copper losses), brush arcing, and bearing friction, the mechanical output power is always lower than the electrical input power:

$$\eta = \frac{P_{\text{out}}}{P_{\text{in}}} \times 100\%$$

* $\eta$ = **Power efficiency percentage**, ranging typically between $65\%$ and $78\%$ for a high-quality 775 motor frame under optimal load conditions.

## 6. Summary Summary Checklist

1. **Physical Form Factor:** The 775 motor is a standardized brushed DC motor size class known for high power density and rugged construction.
2. **Permanent Magnet Fields:** It uses permanent magnets in the outer stator housing to establish a steady magnetic field ($B$) without drawing extra excitation current.
3. **Mechanical Commutation:** It relies on spring-loaded carbon brushes rubbing against copper split-ring commutator segments to reverse coil current direction every half-turn.
4. **Integrated Air Cooling:** Features built-in cooling fan blades that draw air through housing vents, allowing it to survive high power spikes.
5. **Lifespan Limitations:** The continuous friction between the carbon brushes and copper commutator creates physical wear, meaning brushes will eventually require replacement or maintenance.
6. **Electrical Noise (EMI):** Physical arcing at the brush interface generates high electromagnetic noise, typically requiring decoupling capacitors across its terminals.
7. **Input Power Calculations:** Electrical input wattage is the direct product of terminal voltage and line current ($P_{\text{in}} = V_s \cdot I_{\text{line}}$).
8. **Mechanical Output Power:** Output shaft power is calculated by multiplying mechanical torque by angular velocity ($P_{\text{out}} = \tau \cdot \omega$).
9. **RPM to Angular Velocity:** Shaft speeds must be converted from RPM to radians per second ($\omega = \frac{\text{RPM} \cdot 2\pi}{60}$) to perform correct wattage derivations.
10. **Winding Selection Targets:** The 775 chassis is factory-wound in high-speed variants (up to 20,000 RPM) or high-torque variants based on application demands.
