# 07.03 - AC Motors: Operational Principles, Rotating Magnetic Fields, and Synchronous Mechanics

## 1. Theoretical Foundations of Alternating Current Metrology

An Alternating Current (AC) motor converts electrical energy from an alternating current supply into mechanical 
rotational energy. Unlike Direct Current (DC) motors, which rely on mechanical commutators and brushes to invert 
armature current dynamically, AC motors exploit the inherent sinusoidal wave characteristics of the AC power grid 
to establish rotation.

The operational foundation of all AC motors rests on **Faraday's Law of Electromagnetic Induction** and 
**Lorentz Force Mechanics**. When an alternating current flows through a stationary set of windings, it generates 
a time-varying magnetic flux ($\Phi$). If this flux passes through a closed conductive loop inside the rotor, it 
induces an Electromotive Force (EMF), denoted as $\mathcal{E}$:

$$\mathcal{E} = -N \frac{d\Phi}{dt}$$

Where $N$ represents the number of winding turns. The resulting induced current ($I$) interacts with the external 
magnetic field ($\mathbf{B}$) to generate a mechanical torque ($\mathbf{\tau}$). This interaction is governed by 
the vector cross product:

$$\mathbf{\tau} = \mathbf{r} \times \mathbf{F} = \mathbf{r} \times \left( I \oint d\mathbf{l} \times \mathbf{B} \right)$$

Where $\mathbf{r}$ is the rotor radius vector and $\mathbf{l}$ is the length vector of the conductor path.

To determine the instantaneous direction of the mechanical force vector acting on a single rotor conductor segment, 
engineers apply **Fleming's Left-Hand Rule**. This rule maps three mutually orthogonal spatial vectors

## 3. The Rotating Magnetic Field (RMF) Phenomenon

The definitive breakthrough of AC motor design is the elimination of mechanical switching by producing a 
**Rotating Magnetic Field (RMF)** within the stationary stator frame. This is achieved by distributing 
three separate phase windings spatially around the stator core, separated by a physical angle of 120°.

```text
> THREE-PHASE STATOR GEOMETRY AND PHASE DISPLACEMENT

                          Phase A Winding (0°)
                                  ███
                                   ▲
                                   │
                                   │
            Phase C Winding ◄──────┼──────► Phase B Winding
                (240°)             │            (120°)
                                   │
                                   ▼
                                  ███

```

When balanced three-phase AC currents—which are temporally offset from one another by 120° electrical degrees—are 
injected into these spatially displaced windings, their instantaneous magnetic fields combine vectorially. The temporal 
current functions are defined as:

$$I_A = I_{\text{max}}\sin(\omega t)$$

$$I_B = I_{\text{max}}\sin(\omega t - 120^\circ)$$

$$I_C = I_{\text{max}}\sin(\omega t - 240^\circ)$$

At any given instant in time ($t$), the net magnetic flux vector ($\mathbf{B}_{\text{net}}$) is the vector sum of the 
phase fields:

$$\mathbf{B}_{\text{net}} = \mathbf{B}_A + \mathbf{B}_B + \mathbf{B}_C$$

Evaluating this vector sum at consecutive time intervals reveals that the amplitude of the resulting magnetic field remains 
completely constant, while its spatial orientation rotates smoothly through a full 360° circle for every electrical supply cycle.

```text
> VECTOR EVALUATION OF RMF DURING ONE SINGLE SUPPLY CYCLE

Supply Angle (ωt)   │ Net Flux Magnitude    │ Spatial Angle Vector  │ Active Peak Node
                    │ (B_net Resultant)     │ Direction (θ)         │ Driving Flux
────────────────────│───────────────────────│───────────────────────│───────────────────────
0°                  │ 1.5 * B_max           │ 0° (Vertical Up)      │ Phase A Maximum
────────────────────│───────────────────────│───────────────────────│───────────────────────
60°                 │ 1.5 * B_max           │ 60° (Clockwise Shift) │ Phases A & B Balanced
────────────────────│───────────────────────│───────────────────────│───────────────────────
120°                │ 1.5 * B_max           │ 120°                  │ Phase B Maximum
────────────────────│───────────────────────│───────────────────────│───────────────────────
180°                │ 1.5 * B_max           │ 180° (Vertical Down)  │ Phase A Inverse Peak
────────────────────│───────────────────────│───────────────────────│───────────────────────
240°                │ 1.5 * B_max           │ 240°                  │ Phase C Maximum
────────────────────│───────────────────────│───────────────────────│───────────────────────
300°                │ 1.5 * B_max           │ 300°                  │ Phases C & A Balanced

```

$$\mathbf{B}_{\text{net}} = \frac{3}{2} B_{\text{max}}$$

This constant-amplitude, continuously spinning magnetic vector drags the internal rotor along with it, creating 
mechanical rotation without requiring sliding electrical contacts.

## 4. The Synchronous Speed Parameter

The rotational velocity of the rotating magnetic field is designated as the **Synchronous Speed** ($n_s$), measured 
in revolutions per minute (RPM). This speed is determined by two physical constraints: the electrical frequency of 
the AC supply ($f$) and the internal mechanical arrangement of the stator poles ($p$).

$$n_s = \frac{120 \cdot f}{p}$$

To convert this value into angular velocity ($\omega_s$) in radians per second, the equation is modified:

$$\omega_s = \frac{2\pi \cdot n_s}{60} = \frac{4\pi \cdot f}{p}$$

```text
> SYNCHRONOUS SPEED CORRELATION ARRAY (At Standard 50Hz / 60Hz Grids)

Total Pole Count (p)│ Stator Coil Geometry  │ Speed at 50Hz Grid    │ Speed at 60Hz Grid
                    │ Layout Per Phase      │ (Industrial Standard) │ (North American Std)
────────────────────│───────────────────────│───────────────────────│───────────────────────
2 Poles             │ 1 Pair (180° Spacing) │ 3000 RPM              │ 3600 RPM
────────────────────│───────────────────────│───────────────────────│───────────────────────
4 Poles             │ 2 Pairs (90° Spacing) │ 1500 RPM              │ 1800 RPM
────────────────────│───────────────────────│───────────────────────│───────────────────────
6 Poles             │ 3 Pairs (60° Spacing) │ 1000 RPM              │ 1200 RPM
────────────────────│───────────────────────│───────────────────────│───────────────────────
8 Poles             │ 4 Pairs (45° Spacing) │ 750 RPM               │ 900 RPM

```

Changing the physical speed of an AC motor requires altering either the supply frequency using a Variable Frequency 
Drive (VFD), or the physical layout of the stator windings to modify the pole count.

### 5.1 Induction (Asynchronous) Motors

In an induction motor, the rotor receives its electrical power exclusively through electromagnetic induction, 
eliminating the need for brushes.

* **The Squirrel Cage Rotor:** This architecture features solid copper or aluminum bars short-circuited at 
both ends by heavy conductive rings, forming a rugged cage assembly.

```text
> SQUIRREL CAGE CONDUCTION GEOMETRY

           Short-Circuit End Ring
               ┌───────────┐
            ┌──┤  ███████  ├──────────────────────────────────┐
            │  └─────┬─────┘                                  │
            │        │  Longitudinal Solid Copper Bars        │
            │        ├──────────────────────────────────────┐ │
            │        ├──────────────────────────────────────┐ ├─► [ Rotor Shaft ]
            │        ├──────────────────────────────────────┐ │
            │        │                                      │ │
            └──┤  ███████  ├──────────────────────────────────┘
               └───────────┘
           Short-Circuit End Ring

```

To ensure torque generation, the mechanical velocity of the rotor ($n_r$) must always lag behind the synchronous 
speed ($n_s$) of the stator field. If the rotor were to spin at synchronous speed, there would be no relative 
velocity ($v_{\text{rel}} = 0$) between the conductors and the field:

$$\frac{d\Phi}{dt} = 0 \implies \mathcal{E} = 0 \implies I_{\text{rotor}} = 0$$

Without an induced current, the electromagnetic force drops to zero, causing the rotor to slow down. This fractional 
lag is defined as the **Slip** ($s$), expressed as a percentage:

$$s = \frac{n_s - n_r}{n_s} \times 100\%$$

The operating speed of an induction motor can be calculated directly using its slip value:

$$n_r = n_s(1 - s) = \frac{120 \cdot f}{p}(1 - s)$$

### 5.2 Synchronous Motors

In a synchronous motor, the rotor spins at the exact same velocity as the rotating magnetic field ($s = 0$). To achieve 
this, the rotor cannot rely on induction. Instead, it uses a permanent magnet or an independent direct current excitation 
source to lock its internal magnetic field directly onto the spinning stator field.

```text
> SYNCHRONOUS MAGNETIC LOCK PROFILE

      Stator RMF Pole            Rotor Magnetic Pole
     ┌───────────────┐            ┌───────────────┐
     │   [ North ]   │  ◄──🧲──►  │   [ South ]   │  ──► (Spins at exact n_s)
     └───────────────┘            └───────────────┘

```

Because the rotor is locked directly to the stator field, it runs at an unvarying speed regardless of mechanical load 
variations, up to its maximum pull-out torque limit.

## 6. Equivalent Circuit Modeling and Core Formula Structures

To analyze and predict the efficiency, power factor, and torque curves of an induction motor, engineers use the Steinmetz

### 6.1 Parameter Definitions

* $R_s, X_s$: Stator winding resistance and leakage reactance.
* $R_r, X_r$: Rotor resistance and leakage reactance referred to the stator side.
* $R_c, X_m$: Core loss shunt resistance and magnetizing air-gap reactance.
* $s$: Fractional operational machine slip.

The total mechanical power developed ($P_{\text{mech}}$) within the rotor structure can be isolated by splitting the 
rotor resistance term into its electrical loss and mechanical conversion components:

$$\frac{R_r}{s} = R_r + R_r\left(\frac{1-s}{s}\right)$$

$$P_{\text{mech}} = 3 \cdot I_r^2 \cdot R_r\left(\frac{1-s}{s}\right)$$

## 8. Summary Summary Checklist

1. **AC Current Conversion:** AC motors convert alternating electrical current into mechanical rotation by utilizing alternating sinusoidal waves rather than mechanical commutators.
2. **Faraday Induction Laws:** All induction motors rely on Faraday's law ($\mathcal{E} = -N \frac{d\Phi}{dt}$) to induce currents in the rotor across the stator's air gap.
3. **Fleming Vector Geometry:** Fleming's left-hand rule maps the mechanical force vector ($\mathbf{F} = I(\mathbf{L} \times \mathbf{B})$) perpendicularly across the current and magnetic field lines.
4. **Rotating Field Phenomenon:** Injecting balanced three-phase AC currents into coils spaced 120° apart creates a constant-amplitude Rotating Magnetic Field (RMF).
5. **RMF Resultant Multiplier:** The net magnetic field vector resulting from a balanced three-phase stator configuration remains locked at a magnitude of $\frac{3}{2} B_{\text{max}}$.
6. **Synchronous Speed Limits:** The maximum speed of an RMF is governed by frequency and pole count ($n_s = \frac{120f}{p}$); it can only be altered by changing frequency or wind layout.
7. **Induction Machine Slip:** Induction motors require a fractional speed lag called slip ($s = \frac{n_s - n_r}{n_s}$) to induce voltage and current into the rotor cage.
8. **Synchronous Zero-Slip Lock:** Synchronous motors run with zero slip ($s=0$) because their permanent-magnet or DC-excited rotors lock directly onto the stator's RMF.
9. **Steinmetz Power Breakdown:** The Steinmetz equivalent circuit models mechanical power delivery by splitting rotor resistance into thermal losses ($R_r$) and conversion power ($R_r\frac{1-s}{s}$).
10. **Single-Phasing Failures:** Losing a single phase on a running 3-phase motor causes the remaining phases to draw excessive current, requiring fast protection to prevent thermal failure.
