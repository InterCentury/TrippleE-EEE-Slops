# 07.06 - Motor Specifications: Reading Nameplates, Parameter Interpretation, and Selection Strategies

## 1. Introduction to Motor Specifications

A **Motor Specification Sheet** or **Nameplate** is a permanent metal plate or documentation file attached to an electric motor that lists its critical electrical, mechanical, and thermal limits. These parameters are determined through standardized factory testing and dictate how a motor must be powered, loaded, and cooled to operate safely without internal insulation failure.

```text
> TYPICAL INDUSTRIAL MOTOR NAMEPLATE LAYOUT

  ┌──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
  │ INDUSTRIAL MOTOR CORP.          3~ MOTOR                     │
  │ MODEL: IM-775X4                 FRAME: 56C                   │
  │ HP: 2.0         kW: 1.5         PHASE: 3       HZ: 60        │
  │ RPM: 1725       VOLTS: 230/460  AMPS: 5.6/2.8                │
  │ DESIGN: B       AMB: 40°C       DUTY: CONT     EFF: 85.5%    │
  │ INS CLASS: F    PF: 0.82        ENCL: TEFC     NEMA NOM      │
  │ SERIAL: 2026-0528               BRNG: 6203-2RS               │
  └──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘

```

## 2. Core Technical Parameters Decoded

When reading a motor specification sheet, parameters are split into electrical input values, mechanical output values, and environmental boundaries.

### 2.1 Electrical Input Parameters

* **Voltage ($V_s$ or $\text{V}$):** The nominal operating voltage required by the motor. Measured in Volts ($\text{V}$). For 3-phase motors, this is often a dual voltage rating (e.g., $230/460\text{V}$) indicating how the internal winding coils can be jumpered in Star or Delta configurations.
* **Full-Load Amperes ($\text{FLA}$ or $I_{\text{line}}$):** The steady-state current drawn by the motor from the supply line when operating at its full rated torque and power capacity. Measured in Amperes ($\text{A}$).
* **Frequency ($f$):** The design frequency of the AC supply grid, measured in Hertz ($\text{Hz}$), typically $50\text{Hz}$ or $60\text{Hz}$.
* **Power Factor ($\text{PF}$ or $\cos\theta$):** The ratio of real power ($P$) absorbed by the motor to the apparent power ($S$) flowing in the circuit, expressed as a decimal between $0$ and $1$:

$$\text{PF} = \frac{P}{S} = \frac{\text{Real Power (W)}}{\text{Apparent Power (VA)}}$$

### 2.2 Mechanical Output Parameters

* **Rated Speed ($n_{\text{base}}$ or $\text{RPM}$):** The actual mechanical rotational speed of the output shaft when the motor is delivering its full rated torque under nominal voltage and frequency conditions. Measured in Revolutions Per Minute ($\text{RPM}$).
* **Rated Torque ($\tau$):** The mechanical twisting force generated at the shaft during continuous operation. Measured in Newton-meters ($\text{N}\cdot\text{m}$) or Foot-pounds ($\text{lb}\cdot\text{ft}$).
* **Rated Output Power ($P_{\text{out}}$):** The actual mechanical wattage delivered through the shaft. Measured in Watts ($\text{W}$), Kilowatts ($\text{kW}$), or Horsepower ($\text{HP}$).

$$\text{Mechanical Power Conversion: } 1\text{ HP} = 746\text{ Watts} = 0.746\text{ kW}$$

## 3. Efficiency, Losses, and Wattage Calculations

A motor acts as a transducer converting electrical input power into mechanical output power. This conversion process is never perfectly efficient because energy is lost as heat inside the machine.

```text
> MOTOR ENERGY FLOW AND LOSS PROFILE

  [ Electrical Input Power (Pin) ] ───► [ MOTOR HOUSING ] ───► [ Mechanical Output Power (Pout) ]
                                                │
                          ┌─────────────────────┴─────────────────────┐
                          ▼                                           ▼
                 [ Core/Iron Losses ]                        [ Copper Losses ]
                 Hysteresis & Eddy Current                   I²R heating inside
                 currents in stator steel.                   the copper windings.

```

### 3.1 Mathematical Derivations for Power and Efficiency

To evaluate a motor's performance from its specification data, use the following interrelated formula set:

#### Electrical Input Power ($P_{\text{in}}$)

For a Direct Current (DC) motor:

$$P_{\text{in}} = V_s \cdot I_{\text{line}}$$

* $P_{\text{in}}$ = **Electrical input power**, measured in Watts ($\text{W}$).
* $V_s$ = **Terminal supply voltage**, measured in Volts ($\text{V}$).
* $I_{\text{line}}$ = **Line current** drawn from the source, measured in Amperes ($\text{A}$).

For a Three-Phase AC motor:

$$P_{\text{in}} = \sqrt{3} \cdot V_{\text{line}} \cdot I_{\text{line}} \cdot \text{PF}$$

* $\sqrt{3}$ = **Three-phase line constant** (approximately $1.732$).
* $V_{\text{line}}$ = **Line-to-line voltage**, measured in Volts ($\text{V}$).
* $I_{\text{line}}$ = **Line current**, measured in Amperes ($\text{A}$).
* $\text{PF}$ = **Power factor** value ($\cos\theta$) listed on the nameplate.

#### Mechanical Output Power ($P_{\text{out}}$)

The mechanical power produced at the spinning shaft is calculated using torque and angular velocity:

$$P_{\text{out}} = \tau \cdot \omega$$

* $P_{\text{out}}$ = **Mechanical output power**, measured in Watts ($\text{W}$).
* $\tau$ = **Shaft torque**, measured in Newton-meters ($\text{N}\cdot\text{m}$).
* $\omega$ = **Angular velocity**, measured in radians per second ($\text{rad/s}$), calculated from RPM as:

$$\omega = \frac{\text{RPM} \cdot 2\pi}{60}$$

* $\text{RPM}$ = **Rotational speed** of the shaft, measured in revolutions per minute.
* $\pi$ = **Pi**, the mathematical constant ($3.14159$).
* $60$ = **Time conversion factor**, translating minutes to seconds.

#### Power Efficiency ($\eta$)

The efficiency value dictates how much of the electrical power drawn from the grid is converted into useful mechanical work:

$$\eta = \frac{P_{\text{out}}}{P_{\text{in}}} \times 100\%$$

* $\eta$ = **Power efficiency percentage**, ranging from $65\%$ on small brushed motors to over $95\%$ on premium efficiency industrial induction motors.

## 4. Step-by-Step Motor Selection Strategy

Choosing the right motor by its specification sheet requires matching the mechanical demands of the load to the electrical capabilities of the motor.

```text
> MOTOR SELECTION SYSTEM FLOW

  [ Determine Load Demands ] ──► [ Select Input Source ] ──► [ Evaluate Thermal Environment ]
  Calculate required speed,      Match target voltage        Check ambient temperatures
  torque, and peak wattage.      and phase availability.     and environmental enclosure specs.

```

### 1. Determine Torque and Speed Requirements

Calculate the maximum torque required to move the load from a standstill (**Starting Torque**) and the torque required to keep it moving at its design velocity (**Running Torque**). The motor's rated torque must exceed the running torque, and its starting torque capacity must exceed the static breakaway friction of the load.

### 2. Verify Voltage and Power Constraints

Ensure that the electrical supply on-site (e.g., $12\text{V}$ DC, $120\text{V}$ Single-Phase AC, or $460\text{V}$ Three-Phase AC) matches the nameplate voltage limits of the motor. The power supply must be rated to deliver the full stall/inrush current without experiencing a voltage drop.

### 3. Evaluate Duty Cycle and Thermal Insulating Class

* **Continuous Duty (CONT):** The motor can run indefinitely at its rated power without overheating.
* **Intermittent Duty (INT):** The motor must run for short intervals followed by cool-down periods (e.g., a crane hoist or starter motor). Running an intermittent motor continuously causes rapid thermal breakdown of the winding insulation.

Check the **Insulation Class** letter rating to verify the maximum temperature the internal wire coatings can survive:

* **Class B:** Maximum internal temperature limit of $130\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$.
* **Class F:** Maximum internal temperature limit of $155\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$ (Industrial standard).
* **Class H:** Maximum internal temperature limit of $180\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$ (Extreme duty/High temperature environments).

## 6. Summary Summary Checklist

1. **Nameplate Function:** A motor nameplate acts as a technical profile, listing the absolute electrical, thermal, and mechanical limits of the device.
2. **Dual-Voltage Ratings:** Many industrial AC motors display dual-voltage listings (e.g., $230/460\text{V}$), showing that the windings can be reconfigured for different supply grids.
3. **Full-Load Amperes (FLA):** The FLA rating indicates the steady-state current drawn by the motor when operating under its maximum safe mechanical load.
4. **Mechanical Power Conversion:** Mechanical output wattage can be converted between units using the conversion identity: $1\text{ HP} = 746\text{ Watts} = 0.746\text{ kW}$.
5. **Electrical Input Derivations:** DC input power is a simple product ($P = V \cdot I$), whereas three-phase AC input requires a line constant and power factor multiplier ($P = \sqrt{3} \cdot V \cdot I \cdot \text{PF}$).
6. **Mechanical Power Product:** Output shaft power is the product of mechanical torque and its angular velocity ($P_{\text{out}} = \tau \cdot \omega$).
7. **Velocity Conversion Multipliers:** To calculate mechanical output power, shaft speeds must be converted from RPM to radians per second via $\omega = \frac{\text{RPM} \cdot 2\pi}{60}$.
8. **Energy Loss Distributions:** The mechanical power output of a motor is always less than the electrical input due to $I^2R$ copper heating and iron core losses.
9. **Insulation Temperature Limits:** Winding insulation classes (such as Class B, F, or H) dictate the maximum internal heat thresholds the motor can survive before short-circuiting.
10. **Duty Cycle Allocation:** Operators must match the motor's duty cycle specification (Continuous vs. Intermittent) to the application to prevent premature insulation burnout.
