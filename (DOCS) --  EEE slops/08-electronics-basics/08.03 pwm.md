# 08.03 - Pulse Width Modulation (PWM): Duty Cycle Metrology, Averaging Filters, and Driver Interfaces

## 1. Fundamental Metrology of the PWM Waveform Profile

**Pulse Width Modulation (PWM)** is a powerful technique used to encode continuous analog information or vary electrical power delivery using a discrete, digital square wave. By rapidly switching a digital output line between completely on and completely off states, the system simulates a variable intermediate voltage.

A standard PWM signal is characterized by two primary parameters: its fixed **Period** ($T$) or operational frequency ($f$), and its variable **Pulse Width** or "On-Time" ($t_{\text{on}}$).

```text
> PULSE WIDTH MODULATION WAVEFORM GEOMETRY

  Voltage (V)
      ▲
  V_H │  ┌───────┐               ┌───────┐
      │  │       │               │       │
   0  ┼──┴───────┴───────────────┴───────┴───────────► Time (t)
         ◄──t_on──►
         ◄────────────── T ──────────────►

```

### 1.1 Duty Cycle Derivation

The **Duty Cycle** ($D$) is the ratio of the pulse duration to the total period of a single switching cycle, expressed as a percentage:

$$D = \frac{t_{\text{on}}}{T} \times 100\%$$

Where:

* $D$ = **Duty cycle**, ranging over a continuous control band from $0\%$ (permanently off) to $100\%$ (permanently on).
* $t_{\text{on}}$ = **On-time duration**, tracking how long the pulse remains at the logic high voltage level, measured in seconds ($\text{s}$).
* $T$ = **Total signal period**, representing the time required to complete one full high/low cycle, measured in seconds ($\text{s}$).

The total period ($T$) is inversely proportional to the pulse switching frequency ($f$):

$$T = \frac{1}{f}$$

* $f$ = **PWM switching frequency**, tracking how many full square-wave cycles occur per second, measured in Hertz ($\text{Hz}$).

## 2. Mathematical Evaluation of the DC Average Voltage

When a high-frequency PWM signal is applied to a mechanical or electrical load, the load acts as an integrator, averaging the fast on/off states over time. The theoretical **Average DC Voltage** ($V_{\text{avg}}$) delivered by the waveform is calculated by integrating the voltage function over a single complete period:

$$V_{\text{avg}} = \frac{1}{T} \int_{0}^{T} v(t) \, dt$$

Because the voltage function $v(t)$ equals the peak high voltage rail ($V_H$) from $0$ to $t_{\text{on}}$, and drops to the low voltage rail ($V_L = 0\text{V}$) from $t_{\text{on}}$ to $T$, this integral simplifies to a direct linear equation:

$$V_{\text{avg}} = \frac{1}{T} \left[ \int_{0}^{t_{\text{on}}} V_H \, dt + \int_{t_{\text{on}}}^{T} 0 \, dt \right] = \frac{1}{T} \cdot V_H \cdot t_{\text{on}}$$

Substituting the definition of the fractional duty cycle ($d = \frac{t_{\text{on}}}{T}$) yields the core governing equation for PWM power delivery:

$$V_{\text{avg}} = d \cdot V_H = \frac{D}{100} \cdot V_H$$

Where:

* $V_{\text{avg}}$ = **Resultant average DC voltage** simulated across the target load, measured in Volts ($\text{V}$).
* $d$ = **Fractional duty cycle ratio**, ranging as a decimal value from $0.0$ to $1.0$.
* $D$ = **Duty cycle percentage**, ranging from $0\%$ to $100\%$.
* $V_H$ = **Peak amplitude voltage rail** of the high logic state, measured in Volts ($\text{V}$).

```text
> METROLOGICAL STEPS FOR SIMULATED VOLTAGE SCALING

Duty Cycle (D)      │ On-Time / Period Ratio│ Calculation Derivation│ Effective Output Voltage (Avg)
────────────────────│───────────────────────│───────────────────────│───────────────────────────────────────
0% (Low Rail)       │ t_on = 0              │ $$0.00 * 5.0\text{V}$$│ 0.00V DC (Static Ground)
────────────────────│───────────────────────│───────────────────────│───────────────────────────────────────
25% Pulse           │ t_on = 0.25 * T       │ $$0.25 * 5.0\text{V}$$│ 1.25V DC
────────────────────│───────────────────────│───────────────────────│───────────────────────────────────────
50% Balanced        │ t_on = 0.50 * T       │ $$0.50 * 5.0\text{V}$$│ 2.50V DC (Perfect Median)
────────────────────│───────────────────────│───────────────────────│───────────────────────────────────────
75% Pulse           │ t_on = 0.75 * T       │ $$0.75 * 5.0\text{V}$$│ 3.75V DC

```

## 3. Hardware Demodulation: Low-Pass RC Filtering

To convert a digital PWM square wave back into a smooth, ripple-free analog voltage line for sensitive circuitry, an analog hardware demodulator is required. This is most commonly implemented using a passive **First-Order Low-Pass RC Filter**.

```text
> FIRST-ORDER LOW PASS RC DEMODULATOR CIRCUIT

  Digital PWM Input
  ───[ Resistor (R) ]───┬────────────────► Smoothed Analog Output (V_avg)
                        │
                  [ Capacitor (C) ]
                        │
  Ground Return Line ───┴────────────────► Common Ground (V_SS)

```

The low-pass filter strips away the high-frequency switching harmonics of the square wave, leaving only the stable DC average component. The physical behavior of the filter is governed by its **Cutoff Frequency** ($f_c$):

$$f_c = \frac{1}{2\pi \cdot R \cdot C}$$

Where:

* $f_c$ = **Filter cutoff frequency**, representing the $-3\text{dB}$ attenuation boundary, measured in Hertz ($\text{Hz}$).
* $R$ = **Filter resistor value**, measured in Ohms ($\Omega$).
* $C$ = **Filter capacitor value**, measured in Farads ($\text{F}$).

### 3.1 Frequency Selection Constraints

To ensure proper smoothing and remove unwanted voltage ripples, the PWM switching frequency ($f_{\text{pwm}}$) must be significantly higher than the filter's cutoff frequency ($f_c$):

$$f_{\text{pwm}} \gg f_c$$

If the switching frequency is too low or close to $f_c$, the capacitor will partially charge and discharge during each cycle, causing an unwanted **voltage ripple** ($\Delta V$) to ride on top of the calculated DC average voltage.

## 4. Power Driving Interfaces: H-Bridge Inductive Mechanics

Microcontrollers and digital logic chips cannot directly drive high-current loads like motors or heaters because their internal silicon tracks melt under heavy current. Instead, low-power digital PWM signals act as control triggers for high-power solid-state switching networks.

For applications requiring bidirectional speed and direction control of an inductive load (such as a DC motor), an **H-Bridge Circuit** configuration is used. This architecture features four solid-state switches—typically MOSFETs or IGBTs—arranged in an "H" shape around the central load.

```text
> H-BRIDGE POWER STEPPING SCHEMATIC

                 High-Power Supply Voltage Rail (V_Motor)
                            │
              ┌─────────────┴─────────────┐
              ▼                           ▼
        ┌───────────┐               ┌───────────┐
        │ Switch Q1 │               │ Switch Q3 │
        │  (MOSFET) │               │  (MOSFET) │
        └─────┬─────┘               └─────┬─────┘
              ├───► [ MOTOR LOAD ] ◄──────┤
              ▼                           ▼
        ┌───────────┐               ┌───────────┐
        │ Switch Q2 │               │ Switch Q4 │
        │  (MOSFET) │               │  (MOSFET) │
        └─────┬─────┘               └─────┬─────┘
              └─────────────┬─────────────┘
                            ▼
                    High-Current Ground

```

## 5. PWM Application Architecture Domains

* **DC Motor Speed Regulation:** Controls fan velocity and electric vehicle acceleration. Pulsing power switches at frequencies above the human acoustic range ($20\text{kHz}$) eliminates audible humming or buzzing from the motor windings.
* **Class-D Audio Amplifiers:** Converts audio signals into high-frequency PWM streams. The power transistors operate exclusively in fully on or fully off states, minimizing power dissipation ($P = I^2 \cdot R_{\text{ds(on)}}$) and allowing amplifiers to achieve power efficiencies exceeding $90\%$.
* **LED Dimming and Luminance Control:** Varies perceived brightness by rapidly cycling current to LEDs at high frequencies. Because the switching occurs faster than the human eye can resolve ($>200\text{Hz}$), the eye integrates the pulses via **persistence of vision**, perceiving a smooth dimming effect without visible flicker.

## 6. Summary Summary Checklist

1. **Digital-Analog Simulation:** Pulse Width Modulation simulates a variable analog voltage by rapidly switching a digital line between high and low states.
2. **Duty Cycle Calculations:** The duty cycle represents the ratio of on-time to total signal period, calculated using the percentage formula $D = \frac{t_{\text{on}}}{T} \times 100\%$.
3. **Frequency Definiton:** The operational frequency of a PWM wave is the mathematical inverse of its full periodic cycle duration ($f = \frac{1}{T}$).
4. **Linear Voltage Scaling:** The average voltage delivered to a load scales linearly with the duty cycle, calculated via $V_{\text{avg}} = d \cdot V_H$.
5. **RC Hardware Filtering:** A first-order low-pass RC filter can demodulate a digital square wave back into a smooth analog DC signal.
6. **Cutoff Threshold Rules:** To eliminate unwanted voltage ripples, the PWM switching frequency must be significantly higher than the filter's cutoff frequency ($f_{\text{pwm}} \gg f_c$).
7. **H-Bridge Direction Control:** H-bridge topologies use four solid-state switches around a central load to provide bidirectional control of current flow.
8. **Active Dynamic Braking:** Shorting the low-side switches of an H-bridge configuration channels back-EMF to lock the motor shaft instantly.
9. **Shoot-Through Failure Prevention:** Simultaneously activating both the upper and lower transistors on a single side of an H-bridge causes a catastrophic short circuit called shoot-through.
10. **High Efficiency Switching:** PWM drivers are highly efficient because transistors spend minimal time in the linear region, minimizing thermal power losses ($I^2R$).
