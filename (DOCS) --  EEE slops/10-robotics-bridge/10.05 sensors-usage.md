# 10.05 - Robotics Sensor Integration & Environmental Perception

## 1. The Perception-Action Loop

Actuation without perception results in blind, open-loop operation. To achieve autonomy, a robotic system must continuously sample its physical environment, quantify those physical properties into electrical signals, and process those signals mathematically to make navigational decisions.

Sensors fall into two primary electrical categories:

1. **Analog Sensors:** Output a continuously variable voltage proportional to the physical stimulus. Requires an Analog-to-Digital Converter (ADC).
2. **Digital Sensors:** Output discrete binary logic states (HIGH/LOW) or transmit structured data packets via digital communication buses (I2C, SPI, UART).

---

## 2. Analog Acquisition & The ADC Pipeline

Microcontrollers evaluate binary logic. To interpret a continuous analog voltage, the embedded system uses an **Analog-to-Digital Converter (ADC)** to quantize the voltage into a discrete integer.

### 2.1 ADC Resolution Mathematics

The precision of a sensor reading is strictly bounded by the bit-depth ($n$) of the ADC and the system reference voltage ($V_{\text{ref}}$).

The smallest measurable voltage change (Step Size or LSB) is calculated as:

$$\text{Step Size} = \frac{V_{\text{ref}}}{2^n - 1}$$

To translate the raw integer returned by the ADC back into a real-world voltage:

$$V_{\text{sensor}} = \text{ADC}_{\text{value}} \cdot \left( \frac{V_{\text{ref}}}{2^n - 1} \right)$$

*Example:* Using a standard 10-bit ADC ($n = 10$, max value 1023) operating on a 5V logic rail:

* $\text{Step Size} = 5 / 1023 = 0.00488$ Volts per step (4.88 mV).
* If the ADC reads `512`, the physical voltage is $512 \cdot 0.00488 \approx 2.5$ Volts.

## 3. Ultrasonic Time-of-Flight (HC-SR04)

Ultrasonic sensors measure distance using the physical propagation delay of high-frequency sound waves. This is a **Time-of-Flight (ToF)** measurement.

### 3.1 Acoustic Kinematic Formula

The sensor emits a 40 kHz acoustic burst. The acoustic wave travels through the air, impacts an object, and reflects back to the receiver. The microcontroller measures the total time ($\Delta t$) between transmission and reception.

Because the sound wave travels to the object and back, the total distance traveled is twice the actual distance to the object.

$$d = \frac{v \cdot \Delta t}{2}$$

Where:

* $d$ = Distance to the target object.
* $v$ = Speed of sound in air (approximately $343$ m/s or $0.0343$ cm/µs at 20°C).
* $\Delta t$ = Total microsecond duration measured by the MCU timer.

*Firmware Implementation Note:* At room temperature, to calculate distance in centimeters directly from microsecond flight time, the formula simplifies to: $d_{\text{cm}} = \Delta t / 58$.

## 4. Infrared (IR) Reflectance & Proximity

Infrared sensors are the foundation of line-tracking and short-range obstacle avoidance. They utilize a matched pair: an IR LED emitter and an IR phototransistor receiver.

### 4.1 The Voltage Divider Mechanism

The phototransistor acts as a variable resistor ($R_{\text{photo}}$). When IR light bounces off a bright surface and strikes the base of the transistor, its internal resistance drops. When over a dark surface (which absorbs IR), the resistance increases massively.

This transistor is wired in series with a fixed resistor ($R_{\text{fixed}}$) to form a voltage divider.

$$V_{\text{out}} = V_{\text{cc}} \cdot \left( \frac{R_{\text{photo}}}{R_{\text{photo}} + R_{\text{fixed}}} \right)$$

* **White Surface (High Reflection):** $R_{\text{photo}}$ approaches 0 ohms. $V_{\text{out}}$ drops near 0V (Logic LOW).
* **Black Surface (High Absorption):** $R_{\text{photo}}$ approaches infinity. $V_{\text{out}}$ pulls up near $V_{\text{cc}}$ (Logic HIGH).

## 5. Quadrature Encoders & Wheel Odometry

To calculate exactly how far a robot has traveled (Dead Reckoning) and precisely regulate motor RPM, optical or magnetic quadrature encoders are attached directly to the motor output shafts.

### 5.1 Phase Shift Logic

A quadrature encoder outputs two distinct square waves (Channel A and Channel B) that are physically offset by 90 degrees out of phase.

* By counting the rising and falling edges of the pulses, the MCU tracks **Position/Distance**.
* By evaluating which channel leads the other (A before B, or B before A), the MCU determines **Direction of Rotation**.

### 5.2 Odometry Calculus

Encoder resolution is defined by **Counts Per Revolution (CPR)**. To translate raw encoder ticks into physical linear distance traveled ($D$):

$$D = \pi \cdot d_{\text{wheel}} \cdot \left( \frac{N_{\text{ticks}}}{\text{CPR}} \right)$$

Where:

* $D$ = Linear distance traveled.
* $d_{\text{wheel}}$ = Diameter of the driven wheel.
* $N_{\text{ticks}}$ = Total accumulated encoder counts.
* $\text{CPR}$ = Encoder specific Counts Per Revolution (factoring in motor gearbox ratios).

## 6. Inertial Measurement Units (IMU) & Sensor Fusion

An IMU typically houses a MEMS Accelerometer and a MEMS Gyroscope to determine the robot's spatial orientation (Pitch, Roll, Yaw) and detect physical impacts.

### 6.1 The Noise-Drift Dichotomy

* **Accelerometers:** Measure static gravitational acceleration vectors ($F = ma$). They provide an absolute reference to the ground but are highly susceptible to high-frequency mechanical vibration noise from motors.
* **Gyroscopes:** Measure angular velocity ($\omega$). Integrating this velocity over time yields an angle. Gyroscopes are immune to vibration but suffer from continuous mathematical integration drift over time, causing the angle to wander away from reality.

### 6.2 The Complementary Filter

To achieve stable orientation data, engineers use **Sensor Fusion**—specifically the Complementary Filter—which mathematically merges both sensors to exploit their strengths and cancel their weaknesses. It applies a low-pass filter to the accelerometer and a high-pass filter to the gyroscope.

$$\theta_{\text{fused}} = \alpha \cdot (\theta_{\text{previous}} + \omega_{\text{gyro}} \cdot \Delta t) + (1 - \alpha) \cdot \theta_{\text{accel}}$$

Where:

* $\theta_{\text{fused}}$ = The final, stable calculated angle.
* $\alpha$ = The filter tuning coefficient (typically $0.95$ to $0.98$). It dictates how much trust is placed in the gyroscope versus the accelerometer.
* $\omega_{\text{gyro}}$ = Raw angular velocity from the gyro.
* $\Delta t$ = The cycle execution time.
* $\theta_{\text{accel}}$ = The raw absolute angle calculated from the gravity vector.

## 7. Summary Checklist

1. **ADC Quantization Constraints:** Analog sensor precision is rigidly limited by the bit-depth of the ADC. A 10-bit ADC inherently destroys any signal variations smaller than ~4.8mV on a 5V system.
2. **Acoustic Limitations:** Ultrasonic ToF sensors suffer from multipath interference in tight corridors and fail to detect soft, sound-absorbing materials (like foam or fabric).
3. **Ambient Light Interference:** IR reflectance sensors must be shielded from external sunlight, which blasts the photodiode with wide-spectrum IR radiation and forces a false logic state.
4. **Hardware Timers for Encoders:** Quadrature encoders generate high-frequency pulses. Polling them sequentially in the main software loop guarantees missed ticks. They *must* be attached to hardware interrupt pins (ISR) to trigger immediate CPU execution on state changes.
5. **IMU Drift Accumulation:** Never rely solely on a gyroscope for long-term heading calculation. Integration drift will mathematically guarantee heading failure without an absolute reference (magnetometer or accelerometer) to pull it back to zero.
6. **Sensor Fusion Necessity:** Raw IMU data is practically useless for dynamic balancing robots until processed through a Complementary Filter or Kalman Filter to strip mechanical noise and correct mathematical drift.
