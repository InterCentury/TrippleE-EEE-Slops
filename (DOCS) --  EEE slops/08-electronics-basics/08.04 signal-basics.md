## 1. Fundamental Domain Classification of Electrical Signals

In electronic engineering, an electrical signal is a time-varying voltage ($V$) or current ($I$) that carries information. Signals are broadly classified into two primary domains based on how they track time and amplitude states.

### 1.1 Continuous-Time vs. Discrete-Time Signals

* **Continuous-Time Signals:** Defined for every infinitely small moment across a continuous time interval. Real-world physical quantities (such as an audio pressure wave or a temperature sensor output) are naturally continuous-time signals, mathematically denoted as $x(t)$, where the independent variable $t$ represents continuous time.
* **Discrete-Time Signals:** Defined only at specific, isolated points in time. These are typically generated by taking periodic snapshots of a continuous signal using an Analog-to-Digital Converter (ADC). They are mathematically denoted as $x[n]$, where $n$ is an integer representing the discrete sample index step.

```text
> CONTINUOUS TIME VS. DISCRETE TIME SAMPLING REPRESENTATION

  Continuous Signal x(t)                     Discrete Signal x[n]
      ▲                                          ▲
      │    ▄▀▀▄                                  │    █
      │   ▄    ▄                                 │   █ █   █
   0  ┼──▄──────▄──────► Time (t)             0  ┼──█──█──█──█────► Index (n)
      │ ▄        ▄                               │ █        █

```

### 1.2 Analog vs. Digital Amplitudes

* **Analog Signals:** Possess a continuous amplitude range. An analog signal can take on any real value within its operating voltage limits, meaning it has infinite amplitude resolution.
* **Digital Signals:** Possess a quantized amplitude range. The amplitude is restricted to a finite set of predetermined discrete levels (such as binary $0$ and $1$), mapping to distinct digital logic voltage windows.

## 2. Metrology of Periodic Waveforms

A signal is classified as **periodic** if it repeats its exact geometric pattern at regular, unchanging time intervals. This mathematical relationship is expressed as:

$$x(t) = x(t + T)$$

Where:

* $x(t)$ = **Instantaneous signal value** (voltage or current) at any given moment $t$.
* $T$ = **Fundamental period** of the waveform, representing the duration of one complete cycle, measured in seconds ($\text{s}$).

### 2.1 Mathematical Anatomy of a Sine Wave

The alternating current (AC) sine wave is the fundamental reference waveform in electronics and power systems. It is modeled using the following parameter set:

$$v(t) = V_p \cdot \sin(\omega t + \phi)$$

Where:

* $v(t)$ = **Instantaneous voltage amplitude** at time $t$, measured in Volts ($\text{V}$).
* $V_p$ = **Peak amplitude** ($V_{\text{max}}$), tracking the absolute maximum voltage excursion measured from the zero-volt baseline, measured in Volts ($\text{V}$).
* $\omega$ = **Angular frequency**, representing the rotational velocity of the wave vector, measured in radians per second ($\text{rad/s}$).
* $t$ = **Time variable**, measured in seconds ($\text{s}$).
* $\phi$ = **Phase angle offset**, dictating the horizontal time alignment shift of the wave relative to a $t = 0$ reference point, measured in radians ($\text{rad}$) or degrees ($^\circ$).

## 3. Core Waveform Metrology Formulas

To characterize and compare different alternating waveforms, engineers use a specific set of mathematical metrics that describe voltage thresholds, averages, and power delivery profiles.

### 3.1 Peak-to-Peak Value ($V_{p\text{-}p}$)

Tracks the total vertical voltage distance from the absolute positive peak to the absolute negative peak of the waveform cycle:

$$V_{p\text{-}p} = V_{\text{max}} - V_{\text{min}}$$

Where:

* $V_{p\text{-}p}$ = **Peak-to-peak voltage value**, measured in Volts ($\text{V}$).
* $V_{\text{max}}$ = **Maximum positive peak value** of the waveform cycle, measured in Volts ($\text{V}$).
* $V_{\text{min}}$ = **Minimum negative peak value** of the waveform cycle, measured in Volts ($\text{V}$).

For a standard, symmetrical sine wave where $V_{\text{max}} = V_p$ and $V_{\text{min}} = -V_p$, this simplifies to:

$$V_{p\text{-}p} = 2 \cdot V_p$$

### 3.2 Average Value ($V_{\text{avg}}$)

Calculated by taking the definite mathematical integral of the time-varying signal across one full fundamental period $T$:

$$V_{\text{avg}} = \frac{1}{T} \int_{0}^{T} v(t) \, dt$$

Where:

* $V_{\text{avg}}$ = **Average voltage value** over a full cycle, measured in Volts ($\text{V}$).

> ⚠️ **Symmetrical Waveform Note:** For a pure, symmetrical AC sine wave oscillating equally above and below the zero baseline, the positive area exactly cancels out the negative area over a full cycle, resulting in an absolute average voltage of $V_{\text{avg}} = 0\text{V}$.
> Because an average of zero does not describe the actual energy or work potential of the signal, engineers calculate the average over **one half-cycle** ($0$ to $\frac{T}{2}$) when evaluating pure AC waveforms:
> $$V_{\text{avg(half-cycle)}} = \frac{2}{\pi} \cdot V_p \approx 0.637 \cdot V_p$$
> 
> 

### 3.3 Root-Mean-Square Value ($V_{\text{rms}}$)

The **Root-Mean-Square (RMS)** value represents the effective DC voltage equivalent of an alternating signal. It quantifies the signal's capacity to deliver real electrical power to a resistive load. It is derived by squaring the signal function, calculating its mean value over a full period, and taking the square root:

$$V_{\text{rms}} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{T} \int_{0}^{T} [v(t)]^2 \, dt}$$

Where:

* $V_{\text{rms}}$ = **Root-mean-square effective voltage**, measured in Volts ($\text{V}$).

For a pure, undistorted sinusoidal wave, solving this integral yields a constant scaling factor:

$$V_{\text{rms}} = \frac{V_p}{\sqrt{2}} \approx 0.707 \cdot V_p$$

## 5. Frequency, Phase, and Domain Scaling Metrology

Signals can also be characterized by their frequency and how they shift relative to one another in time.

### 5.1 Frequency and Period Relation

Frequency ($f$) tracks the total number of complete periodic cycles executed per second:

$$f = \frac{1}{T} \quad \text{and} \quad \omega = 2\pi f = \frac{2\pi}{T}$$

Where:

* $f$ = **Cyclic frequency**, measured in Hertz ($\text{Hz}$).
* $\omega$ = **Angular frequency**, measured in radians per second ($\text{rad/s}$).
* $\pi$ = **Pi**, the mathematical constant (approximately $3.14159$).

### 5.2 Phase Shift and Time Delay

When two signals operate at identical frequencies but their peaks do not align perfectly in time, they exhibit a **Phase Shift** ($\Delta \theta$). This horizontal angular offset can be translated into an exact physical time delay ($\Delta t$):

$$\Delta t = \frac{\Delta \theta}{360^\circ} \cdot T = \frac{\Delta \phi}{\omega}$$

Where:

* $\Delta t$ = **Physical time delay offset**, measured in seconds ($\text{s}$).
* $\Delta \theta$ = **Phase shift angle**, measured in degrees ($^\circ$).
* $\Delta \phi$ = **Phase shift angle**, measured in radians ($\text{rad}$).

## 6. Spectral Analysis Foundations: The Frequency Domain

Any complex, non-sinusoidal periodic electronic signal can be decomposed into a compilation of pure sine waves operating at different frequencies, amplitudes, and phases. This mathematical concept is called **Fourier Analysis**.

### 6.1 Time-Domain vs. Frequency-Domain

* **Time-Domain Analysis:** Evaluates signal variations using an **Oscilloscope**, displaying instantaneous amplitude changes along a horizontal time axis ($t$).
* **Frequency-Domain Analysis:** Evaluates signal composition using a **Spectrum Analyzer**, displaying the individual frequency components along a horizontal frequency axis ($f$).

```text
> TIME-DOMAIN OSCILLOSCOPE VS. FREQUENCY-DOMAIN SPECTRUM ANALYZER

  Time Domain (Oscilloscope Display)          Frequency Domain (Spectrum Analyzer)
      ▲                                           ▲
      │   ┌─┐   ┌─┐                               │  █
      │   │ │   │ │                               │  █   █       █
   0  ┼───┴─┴───┴─┴───► Time (t)               0  ┼──█───█───────█──────► Freq (f)
                                                    [f0] [3f0]   [5f0]
                                                  Fundamental Harmonics

```

### 6.2 Harmonics and Distortion Metrics

When a complex periodic wave passes through an electronic circuit, its frequency-domain spectrum features an underlying **Fundamental Frequency** ($f_0$, the lowest frequency component matching the signal's repeat rate) accompanied by a sequence of high-frequency **Harmonics**. Harmonics are integer multiples of the fundamental frequency ($2f_0, 3f_0, 4f_0, \dots$).

To measure the level of signal corruption introduced by these harmonic overtones, engineers calculate **Total Harmonic Distortion** ($\text{THD}$):

$$\text{THD} = \frac{\sqrt{V_2^2 + V_3^2 + V_4^2 + \dots + V_n^2}}{V_1} \times 100\%$$

Where:

* $\text{THD}$ = **Total Harmonic Distortion percentage**, tracking signal corruption levels.
* $V_1$ = **Root-mean-square amplitude** of the core fundamental frequency component ($f_0$), measured in Volts ($\text{V}$).
* $V_2, V_3, V_4, \dots, V_n$ = **Root-mean-square amplitudes** of the subsequent harmonic overtones ($2f_0, 3f_0, 4f_0, \dots$), measured in Volts ($\text{V}$).

## 7. Summary Summary Checklist

1. **Continuous Splitting:** Continuous-time signals are defined at every infinitely small point in time ($x(t)$), whereas discrete-time signals exist only at isolated, sampled index steps ($x[n]$).
2. **Amplitude Contrast:** Analog signals possess infinite amplitude variation, while digital signals restrict their values to a finite set of quantized logic windows.
3. **Periodic Definition:** A waveform is periodic if it executes an identical geometric pattern at fixed intervals, satisfying the identity $x(t) = x(t + T)$.
4. **Sine Wave Modeling:** Symmetrical AC sine waves are mathematically derived using peak amplitude, angular velocity, and phase tracking inputs: $v(t) = V_p \cdot \sin(\omega t + \phi)$.
5. **Peak-to-Peak Voltage:** The total vertical voltage span of a waveform is the difference between its maximum and minimum peaks ($V_{p\text{-}p} = V_{\text{max}} - V_{\text{min}}$).
6. **Sinusoidal Averaging Nulls:** The average value of a pure AC wave over a full cycle equals zero; therefore, engineers evaluate average voltage over a single half-cycle ($\frac{2}{\pi} \cdot V_p$).
7. **RMS Effective Power:** The Root-Mean-Square value ($V_{\text{rms}}$) reflects the effective DC equivalent voltage capable of delivering identical heating power to a resistor.
8. **Sinusoidal RMS Scaling:** For any pure sine wave, the effective RMS voltage is derived by scaling the peak voltage by the reciprocal of the square root of two ($\frac{V_p}{\sqrt{2}} \approx 0.707 V_p$).
9. **Time-Phase Link:** Angular phase differences between matching wave profiles correspond to explicit time delays, calculated via the relationship $\Delta t = \frac{\Delta \theta}{360^\circ} \cdot T$.
10. **Fourier Multi-Harmonics:** Fourier analysis decomposes complex periodic shapes into a fundamental frequency ($f_0$) and integer harmonic overtones ($2f_0, 3f_0, \dots$), tracking distortion via the $\text{THD}$ metric.
