# 12.02 - Short-Circuit Conditions: Low-Resistance Faults, Current Surge, and Thermal Destruction

## 1. The Nature of a Short Circuit

A short circuit occurs when two points that should remain electrically separated become connected by a path of extremely low resistance.

In a normal circuit, resistance limits current to a controlled value. In a short circuit, that resistance drops dramatically, allowing current to rise far beyond the intended design limit.

A short circuit is not merely a wiring mistake. It is a direct violation of the circuit’s intended current path.

```text id="k0r2aa"
> NORMAL PATH VS SHORT-CIRCUIT PATH

   NORMAL CIRCUIT

   +V ─── [Load] ─── GND
            │
            ▼
       resistance present
            │
            ▼
       current limited


   SHORT CIRCUIT

   +V ───────────── GND
        │
        ▼
   very low resistance
        │
        ▼
   current rises sharply
```

---

## 2. Why Short Circuits Are Dangerous

The power supply does not “know” that a mistake occurred. It continues to deliver energy according to the available voltage and the resistance of the fault path.

Using Ohm’s Law:

$$I=\frac{V}{R}$$

Where:

* $I$ = current in amperes (A)
* $V$ = voltage in volts (V)
* $R$ = resistance in ohms ($\Omega$)

If resistance becomes very small, current becomes very large.

### Example

For a 12V supply:

* normal load resistance = 12 $\Omega$
* current = 1A

But if the resistance accidentally becomes 0.1 $\Omega$:

$$I=\frac{12}{0.1}=120A$$

That current is far beyond what ordinary wires, connectors, switches, and traces can tolerate.

```text id="u5m8vd"
> CURRENT ESCALATION

   12V supply
        │
        ▼
   12Ω load  ──► 1A
        │
        ▼
   0.1Ω fault ─► 120A
        │
        ▼
   destructive heating
```

---

## 3. The Electrical Meaning of a Short

A short circuit is often described as a “path with too little resistance,” but the practical meaning is more specific:

* the load is bypassed
* the source sees an abnormal current demand
* energy is converted into heat in unintended places
* the fault path may include wires, traces, connectors, MOSFETs, regulators, or the power source itself

```text id="f8gkz7"
> LOAD BYPASS IN A SHORT CIRCUIT

   Intended path:
   +V ─── [Load] ─── GND

   Fault path:
   +V ─────────────── GND
          │
          └─ load bypassed entirely
```

The smaller the resistance of the fault path, the more severe the fault.

---

## 4. Power Dissipation During a Short

Electrical power converted into heat is:

$$P=VI$$

and also:

$$P=I^2R$$

Where:

* $P$ = power in watts (W)
* $V$ = voltage in volts (V)
* $I$ = current in amperes (A)
* $R$ = resistance in ohms ($\Omega$)

When current becomes extremely large, heat generation increases very rapidly because power rises with the square of current.

### Example

If a short path draws 120A through a 0.1 $\Omega$ unintended resistance:

$$P=(120)^2 \times 0.1 = 1440W$$

That is 1.44 kilowatts of heat.

```text id="h7q1pu"
> HEAT FORMATION IN A SHORT

   High current
        │
        ▼
   Large I²R loss
        │
        ▼
   Local temperature rise
        │
        ▼
   insulation damage
        │
        ▼
   melting or fire risk
```

## 5. Common Short-Circuit Types

Short circuits can occur in different ways, and each type affects the circuit differently.

### 5.1 Supply-to-Ground Short

This is the most direct and common fault.

```text id="e9xkz3"
+V ─────────── GND
```

The supply is effectively connected directly to ground through a very low resistance path.

### 5.2 Supply-to-Supply Short

Two power rails that should be isolated become connected.

```text id="z4qjpw"
   5V rail ───┐
              ├── short
  12V rail ───┘
```

This can force current from one rail into another and damage regulators or converters.

### 5.3 Output-to-Ground Short

A transistor output or logic output accidentally touches ground.

```text id="b3v9ac"
   Output ─── GND
```

If the output cannot limit current internally, the pin or driver may be destroyed.

### 5.4 Output-to-Supply Short

An output node is accidentally tied to a higher voltage rail.

```text id="r0m7lf"
   Output ─── +V
```

This can force reverse current into semiconductor junctions.

---

## 6. Short Circuit vs Overload

A short circuit and an overload are related, but they are not the same.

* **Overload**: the load draws too much current, but still has a useful resistance or impedance
* **Short circuit**: the resistance is abnormally close to zero, causing current to spike much more severely

```text id="m6p2qh"
> OVERLOAD VS SHORT CIRCUIT

   OVERLOAD:
   +V ─── [Heavy load] ─── GND
          high current, but still controlled

   SHORT:
   +V ──────────────── GND
          almost no resistance
          extreme current surge
```

A motor under heavy mechanical load may be overloaded. A stripped wire touching ground may create a short.

## 7. Real-World Short-Circuit Causes

Short circuits often come from physical mistakes rather than component failure alone.

### 7.1 Stray Wire Strands

A few copper strands from a stranded wire can touch an adjacent terminal.

```text id="y8k0xv"
> STRAY STRAND FAULT

   Wire A  ~~~~~~~~\
                    \___ touches adjacent terminal
   Wire B  ~~~~~~~~/
```

### 7.2 Solder Bridges

Excess solder can connect two pads that should be separate.

```text id="v1c9az"
> SOLDER BRIDGE

   Pad A  [ ]====[ ]  Pad B
            solder
```

### 7.3 Incorrect Breadboard Wiring

A jumper wire may be placed in the wrong row or rail.

```text id="p4jw2n"
> BREADBOARD MISWIRING

   + rail  ───────────────
              │
              └── wrong jumper placement
   GND rail ──────────────
```

### 7.4 Damaged Insulation

A cut wire can expose conductor and touch another metal surface.

### 7.5 Failed Semiconductor

An internal transistor may fail shorted, creating a permanent low-resistance path.

## 8. Short-Circuit Current and Source Behavior

Different power sources react differently to a short circuit.

### 8.1 Ideal vs Real Sources

An ideal source would supply any current needed to hold voltage constant.

A real source cannot. It has internal resistance, current limits, thermal limits, or protection circuitry.

```text id="7kngb1"
> REAL POWER SOURCE MODEL

   Supply voltage
        │
        ▼
   Internal resistance / current limit
        │
        ▼
   External circuit
```

The internal resistance of the source limits the fault current somewhat, but not enough to make a short safe.

### 8.2 Internal Resistance Formula

If a source has internal resistance $R_{int}$, then total current can be approximated by:

$$I=\frac{V}{R_{int}+R_{fault}}$$

Where:

* $R_{int}$ = source internal resistance
* $R_{fault}$ = short-circuit resistance

When $R_{fault}$ is very small, current becomes extremely large.

## 9. Thermal Runaway in a Short

A short often causes temperature to rise in the fault path. As temperature rises, materials may change resistance, insulation may weaken, and the fault may worsen.

```text id="l7s9pb"
> THERMAL RUNAWAY CHAIN

   Short circuit
        │
        ▼
   Excess current
        │
        ▼
   Heat generation
        │
        ▼
   Insulation softens / resistance changes
        │
        ▼
   Fault worsens
        │
        ▼
   More current and more heat
```

This feedback loop can damage:

* PCB traces
* connectors
* switch contacts
* transistor packages
* wire insulation
* battery cells

---

## 10. PCB Trace Damage

Printed circuit board traces have finite width, thickness, and thermal capacity.

A short can force more current through a trace than it was designed to carry.

```text id="x0g3v2"
> PCB TRACE HEATING

   Thin copper trace
   ────────────────────► current

   Too much current
        │
        ▼
   trace warms rapidly
        │
        ▼
   copper may delaminate or burn
```

Trace resistance is usually small, but at high current even a small resistance creates significant heat.

### Trace Heating Formula

$$P=I^2R$$

Even if $R$ is only a few milliohms, very large fault current can still create dangerous heating.

---

## 11. Wire Heating and Connector Failure

Wires and connectors are often the first physical parts to show damage.

A connector has contact resistance. Under a short, the contact may heat enough to oxidize, melt, or deform.

```text id="q9f0da"
> CONNECTOR FAILURE

   Current through contact
            │
            ▼
     contact resistance
            │
            ▼
         heat
            │
            ▼
   oxidation / melting / looseness
```

Once a connector loosens, resistance can increase further, increasing heat even more.

---

## 12. Battery Short Circuits

Battery short circuits are especially dangerous because batteries can deliver very high current.

### 12.1 Why Batteries Are Dangerous Here

Unlike a current-limited bench supply, a battery can often deliver a large surge current before its internal chemistry and resistance slow it down.

```text id="n3z1rf"
> BATTERY SHORT PATH

   Battery +
      │
      ▼
   very low resistance fault
      │
      ▼
   huge current surge
      │
      ▼
   heating inside battery and wiring
```

### 12.2 Possible Consequences

* hot wires
* connector melt
* battery protection shutdown
* battery swelling
* internal cell damage
* fire risk in severe cases

---

## 13. Short Circuits in Semiconductor Devices

A transistor, regulator, or IC may fail in a shorted state.

This is especially common when internal junctions break down.

### 13.1 MOSFET Failure Example

If a MOSFET fails drain-to-source short, the load may become permanently connected to the supply.

```text id="x7bv8y"
> MOSFET SHORT FAILURE

   Drain ────┬──── Source
             shorted internally
```

### 13.2 Regulator Failure Example

A voltage regulator can fail and connect input to output abnormally, or output to ground, depending on the failure mode.

---

## 14. Protection Devices

Since short circuits are so destructive, circuits often include protection.

### 14.1 Fuse

A fuse is a deliberate weak point that opens the circuit when current is too high.

```text id="v4n0qp"
> FUSE PROTECTION

   Source ─── [Fuse] ─── Load

   High current
       │
       ▼
   fuse element heats
       │
       ▼
   fuse opens
       │
       ▼
   circuit interrupted
```

### 14.2 Resettable Fuse (PTC)

A PTC device increases resistance when heated, reducing current.

### 14.3 Current-Limited Power Supply

A bench supply may enter current limit mode instead of delivering unlimited fault current.

### 14.4 Circuit Breaker

Larger systems may use breakers that trip under fault conditions.

---

## 15. Why Protection Is Not Perfect

Protection devices reduce damage, but they do not make shorts harmless.

A fuse may take time to open. During that time, heat is still generated.

```text id="t0b7xq"
> PROTECTION DELAY

   Fault begins
        │
        ▼
   Current surge
        │
        ▼
   Heat builds
        │
        ▼
   Protection reacts
        │
        ▼
   Fault cleared, but damage may already exist
```

The shortest possible fault path is still dangerous even when protection exists.

---

## 16. Diagnosing a Short Circuit

When a circuit behaves strangely, a short can often be identified through symptoms.

Common signs include:

* supply voltage collapsing
* power supply entering current limit
* hot wires or components
* visible smoke or smell
* dim LEDs
* repeated fuse blowouts
* unusually low resistance between rails

```text id="r1d8ke"
> DIAGNOSIS FLOW

   Circuit not powering correctly
              │
              ▼
      Measure supply voltage
              │
              ▼
      Check current draw
              │
              ▼
      Inspect resistance between rails
              │
              ▼
      Locate unintended connection
```

A continuity meter or multimeter resistance check can help locate the fault.

---

## 17. Short Circuit vs Normal Low Resistance Load

Not every low-resistance device is a short circuit.

Motors, heaters, and lamps can have low resistance during startup or normal operation. The difference is that these are intentional loads designed to handle current.

```text id="l0m2qx"
> INTENTIONAL LOAD VS FAULT

   INTENTIONAL:
   +V ─── [Motor / Heater] ─── GND
         designed for current

   FAULT:
   +V ─────────────── GND
         unintended connection
```

The presence of a load does not automatically mean there is no short. The key question is whether the current path is intentional and controlled.

## 18. Design Rules to Avoid Shorts

Good wiring and PCB layout reduce the chance of short circuits.

### 18.1 Use Proper Wire Gauge

Thin wire is easier to damage and may overheat under fault current.

### 18.2 Keep Conductors Separated

Maintain clear spacing between exposed contacts and traces.

### 18.3 Inspect Solder Joints

Solder bridges are a common cause of faults.

### 18.4 Use Insulation Properly

Heat-shrink tubing, wire jackets, and board solder mask help prevent accidental contact.

### 18.5 Check Polarity Before Power-On

Many shorts happen during first power-up due to reversed or misplaced wiring.

```text id="8l0mcn"
> PRE-POWER CHECKLIST

   1. Inspect wiring
   2. Check solder joints
   3. Verify polarity
   4. Measure resistance between rails
   5. Power up with current limit if possible
```

```text id="c2w5qv"
> SHORT CIRCUIT EVENT MODEL

   Unintended low-resistance path
                │
                ▼
         Sudden current rise
                │
                ▼
         I²R heating increases
```

A short circuit is not just a wiring error. It is a rapid energy-transfer problem that can destroy the circuit if not interrupted quickly enough.
