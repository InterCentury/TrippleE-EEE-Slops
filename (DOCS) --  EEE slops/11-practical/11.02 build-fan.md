# 11.02 - Practical Electronics: DC Motor Actuation and Transistor Switching

## 1. DC Motor Electromechanics and Inductive Loads

A standard brushed DC motor (often used for cooling fans or basic propulsion) is an inductive load. It operates via electromagnetism, generating rotational torque through the interaction of magnetic fields.

Unlike a purely resistive load (like an LED or a heater), a DC motor presents three distinct current phases that must be accounted for in circuit design:

1. **Stall Current ($I_{\text{stall}}$):** The massive initial current surge when voltage is applied but the rotor is stationary. Limited only by the raw internal copper resistance ($R_{\text{motor}}$).

$$I_{\text{stall}} = \frac{V_{\text{cc}}}{R_{\text{motor}}}$$


2. **Free-Running Current ($I_{\text{free}}$):** The stable, much lower current drawn once the motor reaches terminal velocity with no physical load.
3. **Back-Electromotive Force (Back-EMF):** As the motor spins, it acts as a generator, creating a reverse voltage ($V_{\text{bemf}}$) that opposes the supply voltage, naturally reducing the current draw.

$$I_{\text{running}} = \frac{V_{\text{cc}} - V_{\text{bemf}}}{R_{\text{motor}}}$$

## 2. The Low-Side Transistor Switch

A microcontroller (MCU) GPIO pin typically maxes out at $20\text{mA}$ to $40\text{mA}$. A small DC motor easily draws $250\text{mA}$ to over $1000\text{mA}$. Connecting a motor directly to an MCU pin will instantly destroy the microcontroller.

To bridge this power gap, we use a **Transistor** (BJT or MOSFET) acting as an electronic relay on the "low side" (between the motor and Ground).

### 2.1 BJT (Bipolar Junction Transistor) Calculus

If using an NPN BJT (like a TIP120 Darlington or 2N2222), the transistor is current-controlled. You must calculate the correct Base Resistor ($R_B$) to saturate the transistor and allow full motor current ($I_C$) to flow.

$$I_B = \frac{I_C}{h_{FE}}$$

Where:

* $I_B$ = Required Base Current from the MCU.
* $I_C$ = Required Collector Current (Motor Stall Current).
* $h_{FE}$ = The DC current gain of the transistor (found in the datasheet).

To find the physical resistor value ($R_B$) required to provide $I_B$:

$$R_B = \frac{V_{\text{MCU}} - V_{BE}}{I_B}$$

Where $V_{\text{MCU}}$ is the logic voltage (e.g., $5\text{V}$) and $V_{BE}$ is the base-emitter voltage drop (typically $0.7\text{V}$).

### 2.2 MOSFET (Metal-Oxide-Semiconductor Field-Effect Transistor)

For heavier loads, an N-Channel Logic-Level MOSFET (e.g., IRLZ44N) is preferred. MOSFETs are voltage-controlled. No continuous base current is drawn; the MCU simply charges the gate capacitance to open the channel.

## 3. Flyback Diode Protection (Critical Mitigation)

Because a motor is a massive inductor ($L$), it resists rapid changes in current. When the transistor switches OFF, the magnetic field in the motor collapses instantly.

According to Faraday's Law of Induction, this rapid change ($\frac{di}{dt}$) generates a massive reverse-voltage spike:

$$V_{\text{spike}} = -L \cdot \frac{di}{dt}$$

This spike can easily reach hundreds of volts and will immediately punch through the silicon of the transistor or arc into the MCU logic rail.

To prevent destruction, a **Flyback Diode** (e.g., 1N4001) is placed in anti-parallel (reverse-biased) across the motor terminals. When the transistor closes, the spike is safely shunted back into the motor coil to dissipate as heat.

---

## 4. Speed Control via Pulse-Width Modulation (PWM)

To control the RPM of the fan/motor, we cannot use a series resistor (it would waste enormous power as heat). Instead, we use **Pulse-Width Modulation (PWM)** applied to the transistor gate/base.

PWM rapidly switches the transistor ON and OFF at a high frequency (e.g., 1 kHz to 20 kHz). The mechanical inertia of the motor rotor smooths out these pulses into a continuous rotation.

The effective average voltage ($V_{\text{avg}}$) delivered to the motor is directly proportional to the Duty Cycle ($D$):

$$V_{\text{avg}} = V_{\text{cc}} \cdot D = V_{\text{cc}} \cdot \left( \frac{t_{\text{on}}}{t_{\text{on}} + t_{\text{off}}} \right)$$

* $D = 0.0$ ($0\%$): Motor OFF.
* $D = 0.5$ ($50\%$): Motor receives half power.
* $D = 1.0$ ($100\%$): Motor receives full $V_{\text{cc}}$.

## 6. Summary Checklist

1. **Calculate for Stall, Not Running:** Always spec the transistor's maximum current ($I_D$ or $I_C$) to handle the motor's absolute stall current, not the steady-state running current.
2. **Flyback Imperative:** Never test fire a motor circuit without the flyback diode installed. The inductive kickback will destroy the switching transistor on the very first cycle.
3. **Logic-Level Gates:** If using a MOSFET with a 3.3V or 5V MCU, ensure it is a "Logic-Level" MOSFET (e.g., IRL series). Standard MOSFETs (e.g., IRF series) require 10V at the gate to fully fully open, and will overheat if driven by 5V logic.
4. **Gate Resistors:** When using a MOSFET, include a small series resistor ($100\text{ }\Omega$ to $220\text{ }\Omega$) between the MCU pin and the Gate to limit the instantaneous inrush current caused by charging the Gate capacitance.
5. **Ground Plane Unity:** The high-power motor supply ground and the low-power MCU ground must be tied together. Failure to establish a common ground plane will cause the transistor to float and fail to switch.
