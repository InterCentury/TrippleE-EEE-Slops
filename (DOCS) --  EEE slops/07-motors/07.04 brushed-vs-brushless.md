# 07.04 - Brushed vs. Brushless DC Motors: Structural Architectures, Commutation Mechanics, and Performance Profiles

## 1. Theoretical Foundations of Direct Current Electromechanical Conversion

Direct Current (DC) motors convert electrical energy from a continuous direct current source into mechanical rotational kinetic energy. The fundamental electromechanical conversion rests upon **Lorentz Force Mechanics**. When a current-carrying conductor is placed within an external magnetic field, the moving charge carriers experience a lateral force vector ($\mathbf{F}$), expressed as:

$$\mathbf{F} = I(\mathbf{L} \times \mathbf{B})$$

Where:

* $\mathbf{F}$ = **Force vector** exerted on the conductor, measured in Newtons ($\text{N}$).
* $I$ = **Direct current** flowing through the wire track, measured in Amperes ($\text{A}$).
* $\mathbf{L}$ = **Length vector** of the conductor, pointing in the direction of conventional current, measured in meters ($\text{m}$).
* $\mathbf{B}$ = **Magnetic flux density vector** of the surrounding field, measured in Tesla ($\text{T}$).
* $\times$ = **Vector cross product operator**, indicating the output force is mutually orthogonal to both current and magnetic field vectors.

To convert this linear force into continuous rotational movement, the conductors are arranged around a central rotor axis to generate mechanical torque ($\mathbf{\tau}$):

$$\mathbf{\tau} = \mathbf{r} \times \mathbf{F}$$

Where:

* $\mathbf{\tau}$ = **Torque vector**, measured in Newton-meters ($\text{N}\cdot\text{m}$).
* $\mathbf{r}$ = **Radius vector** extending from the center of the rotor shaft to the conductor bar, measured in meters ($\text{m}$).
* $\mathbf{F}$ = **Lorentz force vector** acting on the conductor bar, measured in Newtons ($\text{N}$).

## 2. Structural Topology and Commutation Mechanics

For any DC motor to maintain continuous, unidirectional rotation, the current direction inside the armature coils must be reversed precisely when the winding crosses the magnetic neutral plane. Without this inversion, the torque vector would reverse its spatial direction every half-turn ($180^\circ$), causing the rotor to oscillate back and forth instead of spinning. Brushed and brushless architectures handle this commutation requirement using entirely different structural designs.

### 2.1 Brushed DC Motors (BDC)

In a brushed DC motor, commutation is handled entirely by a mechanical switching system. The permanent magnets are fixed to the outer stationary frame (**Stator**), while the current-carrying coils are wound around the rotating central shaft (**Rotor** or **Armature**).

```text
    DC Supply (+) ───┘   └─── DC Supply (-)
                       │

               █████████████████

```

The mechanical switching assembly consists of two primary components:

* **Commutator:** A copper ring split into multiple isolated segments, mounted directly onto the rotor shaft and wired to the ends of the armature coils.
* **Brushes:** Fixed, spring-loaded carbon blocks that rub physically against the spinning commutator segments, supplying direct current from the stationary power source to the moving coils.

As the shaft turns, the physical contact slides from one copper segment to the next, reversing the direction of the current flowing into the coils every half-rotation.

### 2.2 Brushless DC Motors (BLDC)

A brushless DC motor completely inverts this physical layout. The permanent magnets are mounted directly onto the rotating shaft (**Rotor**), while the current-carrying phase coils are wound around the outer stationary teeth (**Stator**).

```text
> BRUSHLESS DC MOTOR ELECTRONIC INVERTER INTEGRATION

                        [ OUTER STATIONARY STATOR ]
                      Distributed Phase Windings (A, B, C)
                                  ███████
                                     ▲
                                     │
                 ┌───────────────────┼───────────────────┐
                 │                   │                   │
                 ▼                   ▼                   ▼
           ┌───────────┐       ┌───────────┐       ┌───────────┐
           │ Phase A   │       │ Phase B   │       │ Phase C   │
           │ Coil Slot │       │ Coil Slot │       │ Coil Slot │
           └─────┬─────┘       └─────┬─────┘       └─────┬─────┘
                 │                   │                   │
                 └───────────────────┼───────────────────┘
                                     │
                                     ▼ [ Air Gap ]
                                     │
                             ┌───────┴───────┐
                             │  ROTOR SHAFT  │
                             │  Permanent    │ ◄── [ Spinning Magnet Core ]
                             │  Magnet Core  │
                             └───────┬───────┘
                                     │
                                     ▼
                        [ ELECTRONIC COMMUTATION ]
                       Solid-State Switching Bridge

```

Because the armature coils do not move, they can be wired directly to external power connections. This layout eliminates the need for sliding mechanical brushes. Commutation is handled electronically by an external microcontroller linked to a solid-state switching inverter (typically using MOSFETs or IGBTs), which energizes the stator phases in a precise, repeating sequence to create a step-by-step rotating magnetic field.

To synchronize this electronic switching with the rotor's movement, the motor controller must track the precise position of the spinning magnets using one of two primary methods:

* **Sensored Feedback:** Three internal digital **Hall Effect Sensors** are placed $120^\circ$ apart inside the stator. These sensors detect the shifting magnetic poles of the spinning rotor and report them to the controller.
* **Sensorless Feedback:** The controller uses high-speed hardware circuits to monitor the unenergized phase wires, measuring the **Back Electromotive Force (Back-EMF)** induced by the spinning permanent magnets to calculate rotor position.

## 4. Back-EMF Mathematical Profile and Speed/Torque Dynamics

As any DC motor rotates, its armature coils cut through magnetic flux lines. According to **Faraday's Law of Induction**, this relative motion generates an internal voltage that directly opposes the source voltage. This opposing potential is called the **Back Electromotive Force** ($E_b$):

$$E_b = K_e \cdot \Phi \cdot \omega$$

Where:

* $E_b$ = **Back Electromotive Force**, measured in Volts ($\text{V}$).
* $K_e$ = **Motor voltage constant**, determined by the physical number of winding turns and geometry.
* $\Phi$ = **Magnetic flux** per pole generated by the permanent magnets, measured in Webers ($\text{Wb}$).
* $\omega$ = **Angular velocity** of the rotor shaft, measured in radians per second ($\text{rad/s}$).

The net voltage dropping across the internal armature circuit resistance ($R_a$) is the difference between the supply voltage ($V_s$) and the counter-acting back-EMF ($E_b$). Applying Ohm's Law yields the core armature current equation:

$$I_a = \frac{V_s - E_b}{R_a} = \frac{V_s - (K_e \cdot \Phi \cdot \omega)}{R_a}$$

Where:

* $I_a$ = **Armature current**, measured in Amperes ($\text{A}$).
* $V_s$ = **External supply voltage** applied to the motor terminals, measured in Volts ($\text{V}$).
* $R_a$ = **Armature winding internal resistance**, measured in Ohms ($\Omega$).

Because output torque scales linearly with current ($\mathbf{\tau} = K_t \cdot \Phi \cdot I_a$), substituting the current equation reveals the definitive inverse relationship between torque and rotational speed for DC machines:

$$\mathbf{\tau}(\omega) = \frac{K_t \cdot \Phi \cdot V_s}{R_a} - \frac{K_t \cdot K_e \cdot \Phi^2 \cdot \omega}{R_a}$$

Where:

* $\mathbf{\tau}(\omega)$ = **Output torque** as a function of angular velocity, measured in Newton-meters ($\text{N}\cdot\text{m}$).
* $K_t$ = **Motor torque constant**, dictated by winding configuration and dimensions.

* **Stall Torque:** Occurs when the rotor is held stationary ($\omega = 0$). Back-EMF drops to zero ($E_b = 0$), allowing armature current and mechanical torque to spike to their maximum theoretical limits.
* **No-Load Speed:** Occurs when the motor spins freely with zero resistive load ($\mathbf{\tau} = 0$). The rotor accelerates until its generated back-EMF ($E_b$) rises to match the input supply voltage ($V_s$), reducing current and output torque to zero.

## 5. Thermal Dissipation and Efficiency Constraints

The mechanical and electronic differences between brushed and brushless motors profoundly impact their thermal management and operating efficiency.

### 5.1 Thermal Paths

* In **Brushed Motors**, heat is generated deep inside the spinning inner rotor core via electrical $I^2R$ copper losses. Because this heat is trapped on the moving shaft, it must dissipate across an insulating air gap to reach the outer casing, making brushed motors highly prone to overheating under sustained high-load conditions.
* In **Brushless Motors**, heat is generated within the outer stationary stator core. Because the coils are wound directly onto the metal teeth of the external frame, thermal energy conducts efficiently out to the motor's exterior shell. This direct heat path allows engineers to mount cooling fins or liquid cooling jackets directly to the stator housing, dramatically improving thermal performance.

### 5.2 Efficiency Equations

The total power efficiency ($\eta$) of a direct current machine is calculated as the ratio of mechanical output power to electrical input power:

$$\eta = \frac{P_{\text{out}}}{P_{\text{in}}} \times 100\% = \frac{\mathbf{\tau} \cdot \omega}{V_s \cdot I_{\text{line}}} \times 100\%$$

Where:

* $\eta$ = **Power efficiency percentage**, ranging from $0\%$ to $100\%$.
* $P_{\text{out}}$ = **Mechanical output power**, measured in Watts ($\text{W}$).
* $P_{\text{in}}$ = **Electrical input power** drawn from the supply, measured in Watts ($\text{W}$).
* $I_{\text{line}}$ = **Total line current** drawn by the system, measured in Amperes ($\text{A}$).

Brushed motors suffer from continuous friction losses ($P_{\text{friction}}$) and electrical arcing across the carbon-copper brush interface, which limits their maximum efficiency to between **$60\%$ and $75\%$**. By eliminating these mechanical friction and arcing losses, brushless DC motors regularly achieve efficiencies between **$85\%$ and $95\%$**.

## 7. Summary Summary Checklist

1. **Lorentz Force Foundations:** Direct current electromechanical rotation relies entirely on the Lorentz force equation ($F = I * (L \times B)$) to generate mechanical movement.
2. **The Commutation Mandate:** Unidirectional motor rotation requires regular current inversion inside the armature loops as they cross the magnetic neutral plane.
3. **BDC Rotating Windings:** Brushed DC motors place current-carrying windings on the spinning inner rotor, using permanent magnets on the outer stator frame to establish the field.
4. **Mechanical Commutator Wear:** BDC motors utilize carbon brushes rubbing against copper split-ring commutators to switch current mechanically, creating physical wear and carbon dust.
5. **BLDC Static Windings:** Brushless DC motors place current-carrying windings on the outer stationary stator frame, mounting high-energy permanent magnets directly on the inner spinning rotor shaft.
6. **Electronic Inverter Control:** BLDC motors eliminate physical brushes by using external solid-state inverter bridges (MOSFETs/IGBTs) to switch stator phases electronically.
7. **Rotor Position Feedback:** Electronic commutation controllers track rotor position using digital Hall effect sensors or by measuring the back-EMF voltage induced on unenergized phases.
8. **Back-EMF Counter Voltage:** As a motor spins, its coils induce an internal voltage ($E_b = K_e \cdot \Phi \cdot \omega$) that directly opposes the supply voltage, limiting maximum motor speed.
9. **Inverse Speed-Torque Curve:** DC motors operate along an inverse speed-torque line, where torque peaks at zero speed (stall torque) and drops to zero at maximum velocity (no-load speed).
10. **Thermal Path Efficiency:** BLDC motors are more efficient ($85\%\text{--}95\%$) than brushed options because they eliminate brush friction and conduct coil heat directly out through the external stator chassis.
